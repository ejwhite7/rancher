# Build a business-data licensing readiness plan

A business-data licensing readiness plan should assign owners, define a candidate scope and establish go, revise or stop decisions before transfer. Begin with metadata, then connect rights, exclusions, preparation, quality, security and commercial questions to the evidence each needs. The plan is a decision path, not a promise that completing tasks will produce a license or revenue.

## Set the decision and the effort boundary

Write down what the company is deciding: whether to investigate a defined dataset and use, whether to authorize a bounded sample or whether to accept proposed terms. These are different decisions. A readiness plan should not treat the first conversation as permission for every later activity. Identify the current stage and the conditions required before moving beyond it.

Set an initial limit on internal effort and name the business sponsor responsible for revisiting that limit. This prevents an exploratory opportunity from becoming an open-ended preparation project. Include a clear stop option. The company may learn that the rights position, useful scope or economics do not support the proposal, and the plan should make that conclusion as legitimate as a decision to continue.

## Assign owners by the question they can answer

The business owner defines the commercial purpose and priorities. A system or data owner explains records, field meanings and historical limitations. Legal, privacy and [security](/blog/ai-data-licensing-security-review/) owners address the relevant authority and handling questions. Finance or commercial operations can help evaluate costs, conditions and payment obligations. These responsibilities may be held by different people depending on the organization, but the questions should not disappear because roles overlap.

Record who makes each decision and who supplies evidence. A technical contributor can explain a transformation without approving every legal implication, while a commercial sponsor can support exploration without verifying every [quality](/glossary/data-quality/) claim. Keep those distinctions visible. If a needed decision owner is unavailable, record the gap and limit the next step accordingly rather than allowing another participant’s confidence to stand in for the missing analysis.

## Cross-functional readiness plan

| Gate | Primary question | Accountable owner to assign |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Business scope | What defined decision and effort limit are authorized? | Business sponsor |
| Source inventory | What records and limitations can be explained? | System or data owner |
| Rights and exclusions | Which categories and uses are supported or unresolved? | Relevant legal and privacy owners |
| Preparation and quality | Does the permitted representation support the task? | Data owner and task specialist |
| Security and handling | Are recipient, access and transfer conditions acceptable? | Security owner |
| Commercial decision | Do costs, acceptance and obligations justify the next step? | Commercial or finance owner |

## Build the first inventory without records

List candidate systems, categories, approximate history, known restrictions and important field meanings. Keep the inventory metadata-only. Identify linked sources and attachments separately, and record estimates as estimates. The initial output should make the proposal understandable without distributing the underlying material or unrestricted access credentials.

Use the business-data assessment guide to structure this work and the [rights and readiness](/blog/company-data-licensing-rights-checklist/) checklist to identify evidence needs. The purpose is not to finish every investigation immediately. It is to determine which questions are material to the defined proposal and which owner can resolve them. A narrower candidate scope may emerge from this stage, and that change should be reflected in the plan rather than treated as an inconvenience.

## Establish gates before preparation and transfer

A rights gate asks whether the proposed use can be supported for the candidate material. An exclusions gate identifies what remains outside the scope. A preparation gate defines transformations and their limitations. A quality gate checks whether the resulting material can support the task. A security gate addresses the proposed recipient and handling. A commercial gate considers costs, acceptance, payment and obligations.

These gates inform each other without becoming substitutes. A positive quality finding does not establish [licensing](/glossary/data-licensing/) authority, and a plausible rights position does not demonstrate buyer fit. Record the evidence and unresolved conditions for each. When a change affects more than one gate, reopen all relevant decisions. For example, adding message bodies may change usefulness, confidentiality, preparation effort and handling requirements at the same time.

## Use one versioned decision record

Keep the candidate scope, source references, open questions and decisions connected in a single versioned record. Each decision should identify what was considered, the owner, the evidence reference and any conditions. Avoid a collection of isolated approvals that refer to different versions of the dataset. The plan should make it possible to reconstruct which proposal was actually assessed.

Include the data card or [dataset documentation](/glossary/dataset-documentation/) as it develops. Field definitions, transformations, exclusions, coverage and intended use should agree with the decision record. If the prepared file differs from the earlier description, resolve the difference before delivery. A well-organized approval trail is not useful if it describes a dataset other than the one the recipient will receive.

## Define a sample as a separate project step

A sample can be useful for a specific unresolved question, but it still requires an appropriate decision about scope, recipient, purpose and handling. State what the sample is intended to demonstrate and what it cannot establish about the full collection. Avoid using a small transfer as an informal shortcut around questions that would be important for a larger one.

The illustrated workflow [assessment](/blog/business-data-assessment/) shows how a fictional team can move from metadata to a bounded sample decision without assuming a successful outcome. Use that example to test the plan’s responsibilities and stop conditions. If the recipient changes the use from evaluation to training or requests additional records, treat the request as a change to review. Technical possession should not become accidental permission for a broader activity.

## Make go, revise and no-go outcomes actionable

A go decision should authorize a defined next action with owners and conditions, not declare the whole opportunity complete. A revise decision should state what scope, task or evidence needs to change. A no-go decision should record why the proposal stops and what new information, if any, would justify reopening it. All three outcomes can be useful when they reflect the available evidence.

Before any agreed delivery, confirm that the actual files, documentation and terms match the reviewed scope. Afterward, maintain a register of versions, recipients, [permitted uses](/glossary/permitted-use/) and ongoing obligations. The readiness plan is therefore more than an introductory checklist: it connects decisions to the work that follows. It does not guarantee qualification, acceptance or earnings, but it helps the company avoid moving forward on assumptions no one has taken responsibility for resolving.

## Before you move forward

- Assign evidence providers and decision owners separately where needed.
- Keep every decision tied to the same version of the scope.
- Treat samples and changes in use as explicit project steps.
- Preserve go, revise and no-go outcomes without promising revenue.
